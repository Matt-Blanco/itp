Following my research outside of class (and as I continue working on my thesis) I have been exploring how computation has changed. The focus of my experiments has been through tinkering with an Apple IIGS computer. A 16-bit computer released in 1987.

<div class="col-10 mx-auto my-3">
    <img src="{{ 'assets/images/itp-blog/friction/week4/538A0402.JPG' | relative_url }}" class="col-12 shadow"/>
</div>

The vintage computer has 1MB of RAM, 800KB of system storage, and relies on a BlueSCSI to load software from a MicroSD card instead of relying on a floppy disk drive. With its technical constraints, I wanted to put it in direct conversation and comparison with a modern Macbook Air.

<div class="col-10 mx-auto my-3">
    <img src="{{ 'assets/images/itp-blog/friction/week4/538A0379.JPG' | relative_url }}" class="col-12 shadow"/>
</div>

Previously I was able to hack the Apple II in order to connect it to WiFi networks. A [two-month endeavor that I wrote about earlier](https://mattblanco.me/blog/2026/11/08/old-computers). With that foundation, I first created a simple program to send messages between computers. In essence a two-device chat room. I also experimented streaming my Macbook screen onto the Apple II, but it turned the old computer into a glorified monitor rather than handling any 'real' computation.

When I first mentioned the old computer to people, everyone's first reaction is to try and run a local model on it. With its constrained hardware I don't think it would be as interesting as people think. However, after a discussion with [Arjun](https://arjunmakesthings.github.io/itp-blog/) I thought it'd be interesting to have the two computers talk to one another.

Eliza has been a popular algorithm that has been in brought up again with the rise of AI systems, and because it originated in the 1960s, it would work within the technical constraints of the Apple II. So I put the vintage computer in a literal conversation with my laptop.
